01Where it lives, and why it stayed

The standalone indicator no longer exists

Volume profile, Wyckoff and the zone module were merged into one context overlay, and the profile was later ported into the entry-trigger script. Everything below is read from those live sources.

Three context components were put to the same test — does this improve the decision, measured? Two failed. This one passed, and it is the reason the integration was worth doing at all.

ComponentVerdict
Wyckoff as a vetoBackwards — the vetoed cohort outperformed
Wyckoff as a scoreNull — held-out correlation of +0.013
Structure healthInert — the threshold it needs is never reached
Volume profileWired into the location gate. The value-area low and the point of control count as support, and they fire on roughly 18% of names
Why this one and not the others

The other two answer what phase is this and has structure shifted — both already answered better elsewhere in the system. This one answers where is price relative to where business was actually done, and nothing else in the stack knows that. It adds information rather than a second opinion.

It is a loosening gate — it adds support sources rather than removing them — and it sits behind its own switch so it can be turned off and A/B'd against the prior behaviour.

02What the old guide got wrong

Two of its three core claims do not describe the live code, and the first one matters a great deal.

The old guide saysThe code does
Each bar's whole volume goes into the single bin containing its typical priceEach bar's volume is spread across every bin its range overlaps, weighted by the overlap. A wide bar contributes to eight bins in proportion, not all of itself to one
The value area expands "outward, one bin at a time" in both directionsEach step compares the bin above against the bin below and takes the heavier one. The area grows asymmetrically toward volume, which is the standard algorithm
24 rows40 rows, up to 80
Why the first one changes the picture

Typical-price binning turns every bar into a single point and throws away its range. On a wide, high-volume bar — exactly the bars that matter — it puts all the volume at the middle and none at the extremes where the fight actually happened. The profile comes out spikier and its low-volume gaps are partly artefacts of the binning.

Overlap weighting is more expensive (it is a loop inside a loop) and it is why the heavy recomputation is gated to closed bars — see section 04.

03The three levels

LevelWhat it is
Point of controlThe price with the most volume traded in the window — the fairest price the market has found
Value area highUpper edge of the band holding 70% of the volume
Value area lowLower edge of that band

The band between them is where the market spent its time and did its business. Price arriving from outside tends to be pulled toward the control point; price leaving it with conviction is discovering a new range.

The seventy is not arbitrary — it is roughly one standard deviation of a normal distribution, and it is the convention every desk reading a profile is using. Changing it means you are no longer looking at the same levels other people are watching, which is half of why the levels work.

04How it is calculated

  1. Take the window's high and low and divide that range into rows.
  2. For every bar in the window, find how much of the bar's range falls in each row, and give that row that fraction of the bar's volume.
  3. The heaviest row is the control point — its midpoint is the price.
  4. Grow the value area outward from it. At each step compare the next row up against the next row down and take the heavier; stop when the accumulated volume reaches the target share.

The profile is fixed-range and rolling: it recomputes from scratch each time, always describing the last N bars. It is not an anchored or session profile — there is no fixed start date, so the levels move as the window slides.

The performance rule this taught

The build is a nested loop — every bar in the window against every row — and it is gated to confirmed bar closes. Drawing is separate and runs on the last bar.

That split is not tidiness. The sibling script once gated the same work on “is this a live bar”, which is true on every tick, and it ran a four-thousand-iteration loop per tick until the platform killed the script mid-session — twice in thirty minutes, only after the market opened. Compute on closed bars, draw on the last one.

05Nodes — where price hurries and where it lingers

NodeMeaningWhat price does
High-volume nodeA row far heavier than its neighboursSlows and hovers. Business was done here, so there is inventory to work through
Low-volume nodeA row much lighter than its neighboursMoves through fast. An air pocket — nobody accepted these prices, so nothing is there to absorb an order
The single most useful read on the whole layer

Before a breakout, look at what sits immediately above the level.

An air pocket above means clear road — the move has nothing to chew through and tends to travel. A heavy node above is a ceiling and the breakout will more likely stall in it. Same signal, same bar, entirely different expectation — and the profile is the only layer that can tell you which one you are looking at.

The mirror applies to stops: put a stop below a heavy node, not inside an air pocket. A stop sitting in a gap gets taken out on nothing, because there is no volume there to slow the move down.

06Position, and what the panel scores

The panel reduces the profile to one question — where is price against the value area — and scores it on a four-step scale:

PositionScoreRead
Above the value-area high+3Trading above accepted value — the bullish case
Upper half of the value area+1Inside value, on the better side of it
Lower half of the value area−1Inside value, on the weaker side
Below the value-area low−3Rejected below value — the bearish case

Note the split of the value area into halves. “Inside value” is not one state — the upper half is a market holding its gains and the lower half is one giving them back, and collapsing them loses the distinction that matters.

The distance colouring is deliberately asymmetric

Being above the control point scores friendlier than being the same distance below it. That is not a bias in the display — it reflects that in a long-only book, extension above fair value is a position to manage while extension below it is a thesis to question.

As a location source

In the entry gate, two profile levels count as support:

SourceFires when
At the value-area lowPrice is at or above it and within 1.5% — the lower edge of accepted value, holding
At the control pointPrice is at or above it and within 1.5% either way

Both require price to be at or above the level, never below it. A level you are underneath is resistance, not support — the same asymmetry the panel colours by.

07Against the stages

StageProfile signature
1 · basingControl point sits in the middle of the range; the value-area high lines up with the base ceiling
2 · advancingPrice clears the value-area high on volume, and the control point migrates up as the new range builds
3 · toppingA wide gap opens between the control point and the value-area high; the low creeps up underneath
4 · decliningPrice loses the value-area low and the control point becomes overhead resistance

The stage-2 signature is the useful one to internalise: a breakout that does not drag the control point up with it has not built a new range — it has made an excursion. Watching where the control point sits a few weeks after a breakout tells you whether the move was accepted.

08The workflow

  1. Context first, on the weekly. A year of bars gives the macro fair-value zone every participant is referencing.
  2. Entries on the daily. The best long locations are a pullback to the value-area high from above — old resistance now support — or a test of the control point from above. The worst is deep inside the value area heading down toward the control point: the profile is a headwind and the reward is poor.
  3. Check the fuel before a breakout. Air pocket above is a green light; heavy node above is a stall risk. Section 05.
  4. Take targets off the profile. From the low, aim control point then value-area high. From the high, the classic measured move is the width of the value area added above it.
  5. Sanity-check the stop. Whatever the engine gives you, prefer a level below a heavy node over one sitting in a gap.

Everything here is chart-reading. The profile does not generate entries — the screeners and the gate do — and only its two support levels are wired into a decision.

Window by timeframe — and the trap in scaling it
UseBarsRows
Macro context, weekly25030
Medium term, daily10024–40
Short swing or intraday5015

The lookback is in BARS, not in time. A hundred bars is four months on a daily chart and two years on a weekly one — and two years of business is not a tradeable level, it is an average of three different regimes. Re-scale the window every time you change timeframe; forgetting to is the most common way this layer produces confident nonsense.

Too short fails the other way: twenty bars is one month, the levels sit on top of each other, and none of them mean anything for a position you intend to hold for months.

10Mistakes

MistakeWhy it costs you
Treating the control point as a guaranteed magnetIt is a magnet in a balanced market. A genuine stage-2 leader with strong relative strength can stay far above it for months — and that is the one you want to hold, not fade
Not re-scaling the window across timeframesSection 09. The default is four months on daily and two years on weekly
Ignoring it on the bar before a breakoutThat is the one bar where it pays most — it tells you whether there is road above the level
Reading a level you are below as supportThe gate deliberately refuses this. Underneath a level is resistance
Placing a stop inside an air pocketNothing there to absorb a move; you get taken out on no real selling
Expecting the levels to hold stillThis is a rolling window, not an anchored profile. The levels drift as the window slides — a level from three weeks ago is not the level on the chart today
